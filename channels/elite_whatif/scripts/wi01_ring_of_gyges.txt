If you found a ring that made you invisible, you would stop being a good person within a week. And you wouldn't even notice it happening.

Imagine it's a normal Tuesday in Chicago. You're walking through a park, and something gold is shining in the grass. A ring.

You slip it on and turn the stone toward your palm. You look down. Your hands are gone. Your shoes are gone. You are completely invisible.

Day one, it's just fun. You skip the line at the coffee shop. You walk into a sold-out game for free.

Day three, you stand in the office and hear what your coworkers really say about you. Day five, you walk past an open cash register. Nobody would ever know.

And by the end of the week, the question isn't "should I?" anymore. It's "why not?"

This isn't a movie. It's one of the oldest thought experiments in the world.

Around twenty-four hundred years ago, in Plato's Republic, a man named Glaucon tells the story of Gyges, a poor shepherd who finds a ring that makes him invisible. He uses it to get into the palace, and before long, he has taken the whole kingdom.

Glaucon's point was brutal. People aren't good because they want to be. They're good because they're afraid of getting caught.

Socrates disagreed. He said the person who does wrong becomes a slave to their own wants. Only the honest person is truly free, and truly at peace.

And psychologists see a small version of the ring every day. When people feel anonymous, online or in a crowd, many act in ways they never would with their name attached.
