In psychology, people who build fake scenarios in their head, while listening to music or right before falling asleep, may be doing something researchers call maladaptive daydreaming.

The term was coined in 2002 by psychologist Eli Somer.

It is daydreaming so vivid it feels like a movie. Characters, plots, whole worlds, where you control every outcome.

A car ride with your favorite song. Lying in bed, replaying an old conversation with the perfect comeback. Or rehearsing an argument that hasn't even happened yet.

Here is the thing. Daydreaming itself is normal, and it can be comforting.

It becomes maladaptive when it takes over. Hours a day. Replacing real connection. Getting in the way of work, school, or sleep.

It is not an official diagnosis yet. But researchers around the world are studying it.

For some people, it is the closure they never got. For others, it is the excitement that real life hasn't given them yet.
