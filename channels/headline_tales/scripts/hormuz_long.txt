On a normal day, around a hundred ships sail through a narrow channel of water between Iran and Oman. On October twenty-fifth, twenty twenty-six, just two made it through. For more than eight months, the most important shipping lane on the planet has been almost completely shut. This is the story of the Strait of Hormuz: how a strip of sea just thirty-nine kilometres wide came to hold the world's economy hostage, and what could happen next.

Look at a map of the Middle East, and you will find the Persian Gulf, a long, shallow sea surrounded by some of the biggest oil producers on Earth. Saudi Arabia. Iraq. Kuwait. The United Arab Emirates. Qatar. Iran. There is only one way out of that sea to the open ocean, and it is the Strait of Hormuz. It is about one hundred and sixty-seven kilometres long. At its narrowest point, it is only thirty-nine kilometres wide. And the lanes that giant tankers can actually use are far narrower than that.

In normal times, about one in every five barrels of oil the world uses passes through this channel. So does around a quarter of the world's trade in liquefied natural gas. That oil heats homes in Japan, runs factories in China and India, and ends up as petrol and diesel in cars all over the world. When the strait is open, almost nobody thinks about it. When it closes, everybody feels it.

This is not the first time the strait has been in danger. In the nineteen eighties, during the long war between Iran and Iraq, both sides attacked ships in the Gulf. It became known as the Tanker War, and hundreds of vessels were hit or damaged. Navies from around the world were sent to escort tankers through. For decades afterwards, governments and oil traders asked the same nervous question. What would happen if the Strait of Hormuz was ever closed for real? In twenty twenty-six, the world found out.

On February twenty-eighth, the United States and Israel launched strikes on Iran. Iran answered by turning to the one weapon that could hurt the whole world at once. Within days, Iranian forces declared the strait closed to what they called unfriendly nations. Ships that tried to cross were threatened, and some were attacked. Only vessels approved by Iran were allowed through. The major shipping companies, including Maersk, suspended their crossings. Almost overnight, the busiest oil highway on Earth went quiet.

The shock spread fast. On March third, Qatar, one of the world's biggest exporters of natural gas, declared force majeure, a legal way of saying it could not deliver what it had promised. By late March, the price of Brent crude oil had climbed to around one hundred and eighteen dollars a barrel. In parts of California, petrol passed six dollars a gallon. The Philippines declared a national energy emergency. Sri Lanka moved to a four-day working week to save fuel. Slovenia limited drivers to fifty litres a day. Japan began releasing eighty million barrels from its emergency oil reserves. The International Energy Agency called it the largest supply disruption in the history of the global oil market.

Behind the prices, there was a human crisis that got far less attention. When the strait closed, hundreds of ships were caught inside the Gulf, with no safe way out. By June, around one thousand ships and some twenty thousand crew members were stranded. Many were ordinary sailors from countries far from the conflict, stuck on board for months, far from their families. The International Maritime Organization recorded forty-six attacks on ships by that point, and fourteen seafarers had lost their lives.

As the months went by, the crisis turned into a dangerous standoff at sea. The United States sent warships to the region, and in April, it announced a naval blockade of Iran's own ports, stopping Iranian oil from leaving. Bahrain asked the United Nations Security Council to approve the use of force to reopen the strait, but Russia and China vetoed the resolution. Then came the mines. Over the following months, more than one hundred suspected underwater mines were found and cleared from the shipping lanes. Every one of them was a reason for captains to stay away.

There were moments of hope. In June, after months of talks, President Trump and Iran's President Masoud Pezeshkian signed a memorandum of understanding at the Palace of Versailles in France. Mediators in Pakistan said it meant the strait would reopen and the blockade would end. For a few weeks, the world held its breath. But on July eighth, the deal collapsed. New attacks on commercial ships were reported, the fighting resumed, and the blockade came back. Oil prices, which had fallen to around seventy dollars, jumped back above one hundred.

By October, the numbers were stark. Traffic through the strait was down by about ninety-five percent. Brent crude was trading at around ninety dollars a barrel. And Iran's lead negotiator said the strait would not open until his country's conditions were met. Those conditions include lifting sanctions on Iran's oil, unfreezing Iranian money held abroad, ending the American blockade, and accepting a new shipping route agreed between Iran and Oman.

Which brings us to today. In early November, talks are under way, with Qatar and Pakistan carrying messages between the two sides. Iran says it is ready to reopen the strait within two days if the United States accepts its demands. Washington has rejected Iran's earlier proposals, and it has not accepted these terms either. Everything now depends on whether the two sides can find a deal that both can call a win.

So what happens next? If the strait reopens, hundreds of trapped ships could finally sail home, and oil and gas could start flowing again, although insurers and shipping companies will be slow to trust the route. If the talks fail, the world faces another winter of high energy prices, and the risk of a wider conflict at sea. Either way, the last eight months have shown something that will not be forgotten. A strip of water just thirty-nine kilometres wide can decide what you pay for fuel, for food, and for almost everything that has to be moved.

That is the story of the Strait of Hormuz, so far.
